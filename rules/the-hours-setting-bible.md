# THE HOURS
### A setting for SIGNAL BLEED
*Eleven islands. Eight names. Nothing native to them but the birds.*

---

## THE PITCH

Somewhere in the deep Pacific, well off every useful line, there is a chain of eleven islands running three hundred kilometres from northwest to southeast like beads on a dropped string.

A Spanish navigator charted them in 1587. He named eight of them for the canonical hours — the prayers that divide the day. **Matins. Lauds. Prime. Terce. Sext. None. Vespers. Compline.** He counted eleven islands and named eight. He did not explain the discrepancy, and he did not come back.

Nobody was living there. That's the important part, and it's the thing everyone gets wrong about the Hours. There were no people here to conquer, no kingdom to overthrow, no language to suppress. Fishing fleets and navigators from a dozen places had used the chain for a thousand years as a waypoint — a place to take on water, take a bearing, and *leave*. There are stone markers on three of the islands, weathered past reading. There are no houses. Nobody ever stayed.

Everyone who lives on the Hours now is descended from someone who was **brought here to take something out of the ground or the water.** Phosphate diggers. Cable engineers. Cannery women. Naval fitters. Four, five, six generations of people who were shipped in to strip the place and then were not shipped home.

They built a culture out of that anyway. A hundred and forty years of it. A creole, a cuisine, a music, a way of reading weather, three generations in the same cemetery. It is not ancient and it is not Indigenous and it is absolutely, fiercely **theirs** — and it is going under water in their lifetime, while the last thing anyone wants to extract from the Hours is extracted.

The sea is up. The aquifer is salt. The reef is bone. The tourists left in the thirties and the cable money came in the forties and now the seabed miners are here, and they are drilling the one part of the chart the old navigators always marked and never explained.

And somewhere in the water, or under it, or in the gap between the eight named islands and the eleven counted ones, **the hour that isn't on the clock is getting ready to be observed.**

## PART ONE — THE PLACE

### 1. The chain

Eleven islands. Roughly northwest to southeast, in the order the navigator counted them.

**MATINS** — northernmost. A wet, forested high island, steep and unfriendly to landing. Never developed. Has a lighthouse, automated since 2019, and a bird colony that outnumbers the human population of the entire chain.

**LAUDS** — an atoll. Twenty-two motu on a ring reef. Six are above water at high tide now; there were fourteen in 1980. The people from the drowned ones live on the ones that are left, which is not a solution, only a delay.

**PRIME** — the first place anyone built anything. The 1840s whaling anchorage, then the coaling station. Now mostly a graveyard of tanks and a good place to buy fuel with no paperwork.

**TERCE** — small, dry, and privately owned since 1998 by an entity registered in three countries. There is a strip, a house, and a staff who signed something.

**SEXT** — **the Quarry.** Sixty years of phosphate mining took the island's interior down to bedrock: a moonscape of coral pinnacles standing in a scraped grey basin, visible from orbit. The company town — Calder Field — has been empty since 1979 and is almost perfectly preserved, because nothing grows there to bury it. Its swimming pool still holds water. Nobody knows why.

**NONE** — *(say it "known")* the ninth hour, the hour of the afternoon when the light changes. It is a closed island. It has been closed since 1961 under an order that has been renewed thirteen times by three different administrations, all of whom have cited a different reason. There are still people on it. Their descendants are still on it. Mail goes in. Nothing comes out but the mail boat.

**VESPERS** — the big one. High, volcanic, thirty-eight kilometres long, with a ridge down its spine and a barrier reef and a lagoon on the leeward side. **Port Vespers** is here. Ninety percent of everyone lives here.

**COMPLINE** — the last named island, southeast, low and hooked. The naval station was here. The munitions and fuel bunkers are still here. So is whatever leaked out of them.

**And the three he didn't name.** They're on modern charts as **The Unnumbered** — three basalt stacks southeast of Compline, too small and too sheer to land on, surrounded by water that soundings put at 4,100 metres almost immediately. Every chart of the Hours since 1587, in every language, has carried some version of the same annotation over that patch of water. *Do not anchor. Do not sound. No bottom found.*

The Sounding Company is currently sounding it.

### 2. What was taken, in order

The Hours have been strip-mined seven times by seven different logics. Every layer is still physically present, rusting on top of the last one. **This stratigraphy is the setting.**

| Era | What they came for | What's left |
|---|---|---|
| 1840s–1880s | **Whales and coal.** Anchorage, try-works, a coaling station on Prime. | Stone jetties. A cemetery of sailors from nine countries. The word "try-works" still used for anywhere that stinks. |
| 1889–1979 | **Phosphate.** Sext gutted to bedrock. Contract labour shipped in from everywhere and never shipped back. | The Quarry. Calder Field. Half the surnames on the islands. |
| 1903 | **The cable.** The transpacific telegraph relay landed at Vespers. The Hours became a dot on a wire between continents. | The old cable house, still standing, still owned. The reason anyone can find the Hours on a map. |
| 1941–1946 | **Position.** Naval station on Compline. Airstrip on Vespers. Six weeks of fighting nobody outside the Hours remembers. | Bunkers. Wrecks in the lagoon. A generation of children with names from a country they never saw. |
| 1950s–1970s | **Storage.** The chain became a fuel and munitions depot for a war being fought somewhere else. Twenty-two bulk tanks cut into the ridge above Port Vespers. | The tank farm. The plume in the aquifer. The lawsuit. |
| 1954–1990 | **Tuna.** Three canneries. The fleet. The best decades anyone here remembers, and they were not good. | Cannery Row. The union hall. The recipes. |
| 1961–present | **Registry.** A flag of convenience and a tax residency. You can register a ship here in forty minutes. | Eleven thousand vessels flagged to a place with no shipyard. Two brass plates per doorway downtown. |
| 1968–2031 | **The view.** The Strand. Six hotel towers, a golf course, a submarine tour. | Flooded lobbies. |
| 2040s–now | **The Signal, and the seabed.** Cable landing and data haven; and now polymetallic nodules in the deep water southeast. | You're playing in this one. |

### 3. Port Vespers — the districts

Run these the way the core game runs Rings: each is a mood before it's a map.

**THE STRAND** — the dead resort front. Six towers on a beach that is now a seawall. The water table came up under them: every ground floor is a *second lobby*, ankle-deep to knee-deep depending on the moon, still lit, still air-conditioned, still playing music, because turning the systems off costs more than running them. People live upstairs in the towers now, illegally and in numbers, and take the fire stairs down through the warm salt water to get to work. **Signature image:** a chandelier reflected in eight inches of standing water in a lobby with no staff and the lounge playlist still running.

**LOW TOWN** *(officially Cannery Row)* — the working heart. Three generations to a house, laundry across the alleys, the best food on the chain, the union hall that still meets, the seawall that the neighbourhood built themselves after the Administration didn't. If the Hours have a soul it lives in Low Town and it is not for sale, though the offer is standing.

**THE LADDER** — the old market quarter, stacked up the hillside on stairs too narrow for vehicles. Everything is available. Nothing is on a register. Fixers, chandlers, unlicensed clinics, the night market, the guy who can get a thing off a ship. Smells like frying oil, wet stone, and diesel.

**THE CROWN** *(Government Hill)* — the Administration. Colonial-era stone buildings with modern glass grafted on, the courts, the registry offices with their thousands of brass plates. The Governor's House has a lawn that is watered.

**THE TERRACES** — new. Sealed, climate-controlled towers, positive air pressure, their own desalination, built up the ridge above the flood line for people who bought residency. Their residents do not descend. Their staff ascend daily and are scanned on the way in.

**COLD HARBOUR** — container terminal, fuel dock, the crane gantries, the bonded warehouses. Everything on the Hours comes through one wharf. Everyone knows exactly how many days of food are in the warehouses at any given time, the way people elsewhere know the weather.

**THE LANDING** — Transpelagic's campus at the cable head. Fenced, floodlit, cooled, drawing more power than the rest of the island combined. This is where the wire comes ashore. This is where it comes ashore.

**THE SKYWAY** — an elevated transit line, funded in 2038, eleven stations, never opened. The guideway runs the length of the harbour front on concrete piers. Four stations were finished to the tile. The trains were delivered and are in a shed. At night the guideway is the only straight line on the island.

**WINDWARD** — over the ridge through the tunnel. Rain, valleys, streams that still run, the farms and the off-grid households and the people who left Port Vespers on purpose. The only part of the chain that could feed itself, and it knows it.

**THE TANK FARM** — twenty-two bulk fuel tanks cut into the ridge directly above the island's only freshwater lens. In 2049 something in them failed. The Water Board says the plume is in the aquifer. The Administration says the testing was flawed. Everyone in Low Town buys water in twenty-litre jugs and has for two years.

### 4. Who lives here

About 61,000 people on Vespers, maybe 9,000 across the rest of the chain, and the number goes down every year — the young leave for the continent and send money back, and the money is a bigger share of the economy than fishing.

They are the descendants of everyone the extraction brought: phosphate contract labour, cannery crews, cable engineers, naval fitters, ship's cooks who jumped, registry clerks, tax exiles, and the children of all of them with each other. **There is no majority.** The creole — everyone calls it **Harbour** — is English-framed with a century of everything else in it, spoken fast, and outsiders don't catch a third of it.

The culture is a hundred and forty years old and entirely real: the Blessing of the Fleet in March that has nothing to do with any church anymore, funeral food, three rival brass bands, a way of leaving a light on for someone who's at sea, the tradition that you don't whistle on the water at night, and a deep, load-bearing, unsentimental attachment to a place that was never anyone's homeland and is now everyone's home.

**They are not primitive, not doomed-and-noble, not a metaphor.** They are people with jobs, opinions, feuds, and a functioning municipal government they mostly dislike. Play them that way.

## PART TWO — THE POWERS

**CALDER-VAUGHN GROUP** — the phosphate house that became everything. Dug Sext hollow, ran the canneries, wrote the labour contracts, then converted a century of extraction into legitimacy: property, the registry, the Terraces. Old money that is, on these islands, *the* money. Polite. Patient. On every board. Their family cemetery is nicer than the public one.

**TRANSPELAGIC** — cable and data. Owns the Landing, the wire, and the licence. Sells the Hours' position — the one thing that can't be dug up — as latency. Cheerful, global, weightless, and the only entity here that could leave in a week.

**THE SOUNDING COMPANY** — deep-sea mining, arrived four years ago. Nodule harvesting in the deep water southeast of Compline, which is to say: **in the water everyone has been told not to sound since 1587.** Two ships, a riser, and a collector crawling the bottom four kilometres down. They are very well capitalised and very new here, and they think the annotation on the charts is folklore.

**THE ADMINISTRATION** — the residual government. Technically self-governing, practically a shell operated by whoever is currently paying for the ferry subsidy. Underfunded, not stupid, occasionally heroic in individuals.

**THE WATER BOARD** — the besieged public utility. Chief engineer has been saying the same thing about the plume for two years and being called alarmist. Honest, broke, and correct. Your best ally NPC lives here.

**THE TIDE WATCH** — mutual aid and community defence out of Low Town and Windward. Built the seawall. Keeps the records the Administration lost. Runs the water distribution, the storm shelters, and the phone tree. Not a militia and not a cult: a neighbourhood association with a hundred years of practice and no illusions. **The moral centre of the setting.**

**THE LONG COUNT** — the ones who think the hour should be observed. Not robed lunatics; they're an actuary at the registry, a Sounding Company hydrographer, a retired schoolteacher on None, and a man who runs a chandlery on the Ladder. They believe the chain has been *waiting to be finished*, and they are being extremely helpful to the Sounding Company.

## PART THREE — THE MISSING HOUR

**This is the horror, and it has no owner but the ocean.**

Eight islands got names. Eleven were counted. The gap has never been reconciled and every attempt is in the record: the 1587 chart, the 1889 survey, the 1903 cable soundings, the 1961 order that closed None, the 1974 dive that lost two people, the 2049 tank failure, and the Sounding Company's current bathymetry, which shows a hole where there should be a slope.

The old sailors had a word for what they were avoiding, and the word is the only thing anyone agrees on. **The Stand** — the stand of the tide, the interval when the water is neither rising nor falling. The moment that isn't part of the day.

Everybody on the chain says it without thinking. *It's only the stand. It'll turn.* They have been saying it for a hundred and forty years and it has never once been a lie, and it is not a lie now, and that is not the same as it being true.

The Hours are named for the divisions of time. **There is an hour that isn't in the list.** It has never been observed. Something has been waiting, in the deep water southeast, for someone to observe it.

### How it behaves

Never explain it. These are its *symptoms*, and they're what you put on the table:

- **Time goes missing in exact, repeating amounts.** Eleven minutes off a maintenance log, every night, for six years. A shift that everyone worked and nobody can account for. Two Tuesdays in the same week, both documented, both in the same handwriting.
- **The chain wants to be complete.** Things arrive in elevens. Eleven bowls ordered for a shift of nine. Eleven names added to a list that never gets longer. The eleventh station of the Skyway, which was not in the plans.
- **The stand is when it's closest.** The interval when the water is neither rising nor falling. The pause in the surf. The moment the air conditioning cycles off. Four in the afternoon, when the light changes, which is the hour of None.
- **It comes up through the ground with the water.** The rising water table is not a metaphor here. It floods from below, through the terrazzo, through the cesspools, through the tank farm plume — and whatever is coming is coming the same way. Seawalls are irrelevant. **Everyone on the Hours built the wrong wall.**
- **It is unfailingly polite.** Every voice it uses is helpful. Every offer is generous. Nothing about it is hostile, and that is the worst thing about it.

**Someone taken all the way has gone *off soundings*** — the term for water too deep to measure. Nobody says it unkindly.

### Static, reskinned

**Static** is exposure to the missing hour — time you can't account for, water you shouldn't have touched, a voice on a channel that was helpful. Each mark is a Symptom. Five marks and you are *observed*, and you belong to the hour.

Reskinned symptoms (d6):
1. You are eleven minutes ahead of every clock on the island, and you can prove it.
2. You have stopped hearing the surf. It's still there. Everyone else hears it.
3. Standing water tilts slightly toward you.
4. You know the name of the ninth hour. It hurts to hold.
5. There's a day missing from your memory and you can feel the edge of it, like a seam.
6. Someone at work has started calling you by a name nobody gave you, and correcting themselves, and doing it again.

**The Bleed clock** = the hour arriving on this case's location.
**The Aperture** = the chain completing. Eight islands named. Three unnamed. The season clock has eight segments; the last three are the ones that were never on the chart.

### And what pushes back

The Hours have no ancient guardians and no gods, because nobody ever settled here long enough to make any. **What holds the line is entirely human and entirely recent:** a seawall built by volunteers, a water distribution list kept in a ledger, a phone tree, a hundred and forty years of people agreeing not to whistle on the water at night without remembering why, and a chandler on the Ladder who still marks the old annotation on every chart he sells.

That's it. That's the whole defence. Ordinary people, one generation deep, holding a place that was never theirs, with paperwork and stubbornness and each other. **When the Tide Watch loses a record, the Aperture advances.** Reconnection to that — showing up for the water run, keeping the ledger, going to the funeral — is the only thing in this game that resists Static.

## PART FOUR — TONE KIT

### Sensory palette
Warm salt water indoors. Mildew and floor polish. Fluorescent hum in a corridor with sixty identical doors. Diesel and frangipani. The smell of a hotel that is still being cleaned for guests who stopped coming in 2038. Air-conditioning in an empty ballroom. Chlorine in a pool on an island with no people. The specific silence of a resort at 4 a.m. Rust bleeding through white paint. Bird noise where machine noise should be. **Neon reflected in standing water, always.**

### The register
Not gothic. Not gritty. **Melancholy, humid, over-lit, and polite.** The horror of paradise as a stage set after the audience has gone home and the lights are on a timer. Everything is still working. That's the problem.

### Language
The setting speaks **maritime, industrial, and clerical English** — Harbour creole, shipping terms, radio procedure, registry paperwork. *The stand. Soundings. Off soundings. No bottom found. Bonded. Flagged. Off the books. The turn. Observed.* Names come from the layered colonial record: Spanish canonical hours, English company men, and the surnames of everyone the labour ships brought.

**Never invent pseudo-Polynesian words.** The Hours have their own vocabulary and it comes from work, water, and paperwork.

## PART FIVE — RESKINNING THE OSSUARY SEQUENCE

The three-case season maps over cleanly, and improves:

**Case 1 · ELEVEN MINUTES** → *Ossuary Storage* becomes a **bonded data warehouse in Cold Harbour** running cold storage for Transpelagic. Miren Adeyemi is a night technician. Shaft 11 is a **flooded service shaft** below the waterline, welded from the inside. The maintenance channel voice — *"Grandmother"* — is a **harbour radio channel** everyone assumes is a duty supervisor at the Landing. The eleven minutes fits the setting perfectly now: it was always about a missing hour.

**Case 2 · THE SLEEP STUDY** → *Halcyon Vitality* becomes a **Calder-Vaughn wellness subsidiary** operating out of the **converted ground floors of a Strand hotel** — the flooded second lobby, dehumidified and repurposed into a ward. Dreamless sleep, subsidised for shift workers. The 1,411-name waiting list never changes. The pumps below the ward run continuously and discharge nothing, which on the Strand is ordinary, which is why nobody looked.

**Case 3 · GRANDMOTHER** → the finale runs on **the Skyway**, the **cable Landing**, and ends in the water southeast of Compline, above the Unnumbered, on the Sounding Company's riser platform, at the stand. The work order is signed **G**. Everyone received it at 3:41 a.m. and had already read it.

## PART SIX — WHY THIS IS CLEAN, AND HOW TO KEEP IT THAT WAY

The setting is built so the ethical problem doesn't arise, rather than being managed after the fact:

- **There is no Indigenous people here to appropriate, borrow from, or cast as monsters.** The Hours were a waypoint, not a homeland. Every human culture on the chain is 140 years old, syncretic, and invented for this game.
- **The horror has no cultural owner.** It is not anyone's god, ancestor, or spirit. It is a gap in a colonial naming system and a hole in the seabed — and the thing that wakes it is *extraction*, which is also the thing that built every institution in the setting.
- **The defenders are ordinary and contemporary.** No ancient wisdom, no mystical guardians, no noble-savage register. A neighbourhood association with a ledger.
- **The named cultures in the population are diaspora, not Indigenous** — sailors, contract labourers, and their descendants — which is honest to how remote Pacific outposts actually got populated.

**Guardrails to hold while you write:**
1. **No pseudo-Polynesian naming.** No invented words with Hawaiian, Māori, Sāmoan, or Tahitian phonology. If it sounds like a real Pacific language, rename it. The vocabulary is maritime, Spanish-colonial, and clerical.
2. **No tiki, no hula, no chant, no leis, no "ancient island curse."** The visual language is fluorescent, industrial, and mid-century colonial. Cannery, cable house, hotel lobby.
3. **Don't make the locals mystical.** The chandler who marks the annotation on the charts does it because his father did. Nobody here has secret knowledge; they have habits, and one of the habits happens to be right.
4. **Don't build a real place's disaster into your fiction as set-dressing.** The tank farm and the plume are invented for the Hours. Don't name-check real contamination events, real fires, or real communities' losses in-fiction.
5. **If a player at your table is from the Pacific, ask them how it's landing.** Fictional doesn't mean weightless — the Hours are a composite of real colonial patterns, and someone whose family lived one may have notes worth more than this document.

## THE FIRST THING TO SAY AT THE TABLE

> "There are eleven islands. Somebody named eight of them, four hundred years ago, after the hours you're supposed to pray. He counted eleven and named eight and never came back, and every chart since has carried the same note over the water southeast of the last one: *no bottom found, do not sound.*
>
> Your grandparents were shipped here to dig something out of the ground. You were born here anyway. The water's coming up under the floor, the tank farm's in the aquifer, and a mining company that got here four years ago is currently taking soundings in the only place on the chart nobody has ever taken soundings.
>
> It's four in the afternoon. The light's changing. Somebody has a job for you."
