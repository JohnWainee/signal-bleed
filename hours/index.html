Eleven islands, running three hundred kilometres northwest to southeast like beads on a dropped string. Charted in 1587 by a navigator who named eight of them for the hours you're supposed to pray, counted eleven, and did not come back.

The Chain

Nobody was living here when the navigator arrived. That's the part everyone gets wrong. Fishing fleets and navigators from a dozen places had used the chain for a thousand years as a waypoint — somewhere to take on water, take a bearing, and leave. There are stone markers on three of the islands, weathered past reading. There are no houses. Nobody ever stayed.

Everyone who lives on the Hours is descended from someone who was brought here to take something out of the ground or the water. The chain has been stripped seven times by seven different logics, and every layer is still physically present, rusting on top of the last one.

What was built out of it

A hundred and forty years of culture that is not ancient, is not Indigenous, and is absolutely, fiercely theirs. A creole everyone calls Harbour. A cuisine. Three rival brass bands. The Blessing of the Fleet in March, which stopped being about any church a long time ago. Funeral food. A way of reading weather. Three generations in the same cemetery.

And the habit of leaving a light on for someone who's at sea. And the rule that you don't whistle on the water at night, which nobody can explain and everybody keeps.

Ninety percent of everyone lives on Vespers, and almost all of them live in Port Vespers — strung along the leeward shore under a ridge, between a lagoon that's rising and a tank farm that's leaking.

Common knowledge

These aren't secrets. Every child on the chain knows them. Nobody finds them strange, because they've always been true.

The navigator counted eleven islands and named eight. He never explained the difference, and he never came back.

The annotation

Every chart of the Hours since 1587, in every language, carries some version of the same note over the water southeast of Compline. Do not anchor. Do not sound. No bottom found. Soundings put it at 4,100 metres almost immediately.

The Sounding Company

Arrived four years ago. Deep-sea nodule mining. Two ships, a riser, and a collector crawling the bottom — in exactly that water. Very well capitalised, very new here, and they think the annotation is folklore.

None is closed

Say it "known." Shut since 1961 under an order renewed thirteen times by three administrations, each citing a different reason. There are still people on it. Mail goes in. Nothing comes out but the mail boat.

Number 11

Provident Row runs 1 to 44 and has no number 11. No gap in the terrace, no bricked doorway — 10 and 12 share a wall. The register says 43 units. The 1911 deed says 44. It's a joke you tell people who move in.

Eleven thousand ships

Fly this flag. Not one of them has ever been here. You can register a vessel in forty minutes at the Crown, and the Hours' largest export is the authority to say what something officially is.

The water comes from below

Not waves — the table. It rises through the terrazzo, the drains, the foundations. The seawall Low Town built with its own hands does not help and everyone knows it and they maintain it anyway.

The Skyway never opened

Funded 2038, eleven stations, four finished to the tile. The trains were delivered and are in a shed. At night the guideway is the only straight line on the island.

The tank farm

Twenty-two bulk fuel tanks cut into the ridge directly above the only freshwater lens. Something failed in 2049. The Water Board says the plume is in the aquifer; the Administration says the testing was flawed. Low Town has bought water in jugs for two years.

Where you're from

Provident Row, Low Town. Two terraces facing each other across a street too narrow for a vehicle, running down toward the harbour wall the neighbourhood built itself. Concrete, salt-eaten, patched with whatever came off a ship. Washing lines across the gap.

Built in 1911 by the Calder-Vaughn Provident Society as cannery housing, and never once maintained by them since. Everyone's grandparents were assigned here. Nobody chose it. Nobody leaves.
